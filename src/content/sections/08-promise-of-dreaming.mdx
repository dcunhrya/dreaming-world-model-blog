## The Promise of Dreaming

The promise of dreaming is not just that RL agents can solve GridWorld faster. The promise is that agents may eventually generate useful training environments for themselves in domains where real data is scarce, expensive, unsafe, or slow to collect.

- **Web and software agents** can practice on synthetic tasks — broken unit tests, fake GitHub issues, corrupted configs, API migrations — where rewards come from tests, type checks, and hidden evals. Many real tasks are private; generated variants scale without sharing customer data.

- **Robotics** can train on imagined contact dynamics before touching hardware. The catch: a visually pretty video model is not enough if grasp physics are wrong. The model must preserve action-conditioned consequences.

- **Healthcare** has scarce labels, unsafe exploration, long-horizon workflows, and expensive feedback. Simulated EHR sandboxes and rare-case curricula are attractive — but only if imagined transitions are validated against clinical knowledge and held-out evaluation.

<Callout type="warning" title="Healthcare caveat">
In healthcare, dreaming should not mean letting a model hallucinate patients and then trusting the resulting policy. It should mean building **constrained, auditable training environments** where synthetic rollouts are checked against real data distributions and safety constraints.

Healthcare does not need unconstrained dream worlds. It needs **verified clinical sandboxes**.
</Callout>

This is the healthcare version of the GridWorld failure mode. If the world model invents a shortcut, the agent will learn it. In a game, that produces a funny policy. In medicine, it produces unsafe behavior. The central research problem is not simply generating more environments — it is validating which imagined transitions are safe enough to learn from.
